# Opportunity comparison

When a trio has a set of approved opportunities, the next step is to compare them against the product outcome to decide which one to pursue. Torres covers this in "Continuous Discovery Habits" chapter 7 ("Prioritizing Opportunities, Not Solutions").

The comparison is a structured conversation, not a scoring exercise. The trio looks at each opportunity through five lenses, grounds each assessment in customer evidence, and surfaces where evidence is missing.

## The five criteria

Four derive from Torres's prioritization principles in CDH ch 7. One (outcome alignment) ties the comparison to the trio's specific product outcome.

**Outcome alignment.** Does solving this opportunity move the trio's product outcome? How directly would the opportunity's pain, when relieved, shift the outcome's metric or behavior?

**Customer importance.** How strongly do the customers feel this pain? Intensity, recurrence, and cross-customer signal in the interview quotes.

**Market size / frequency.** How many customers experience this pain, and how often? Frequency multiplies importance: a moderately painful experience that happens daily can outweigh a sharp pain that happens once a year.

**Strategic fit.** Does solving this opportunity align with the team's vision and the product's positioning? Torres calls this "alignment with vision".

**Competitive landscape.** Does solving this opportunity differentiate against alternatives the customer could choose? For internal products with no external competition, this criterion often does not apply.

## How to score

Use qualitative language, not numbers. Torres is explicit about this: numeric scoring invites summing, and summing collapses the nuance the comparison is meant to surface. A trio that sums scores will pick the "highest number" instead of reasoning about trade-offs.

For each criterion per opportunity, the trio lands on one of:

- **Strong** when the interview evidence clearly supports a high score
- **Medium** when the evidence is partial or comes with caveats
- **Weak** when the evidence suggests low relevance, but the opportunity is still a real candidate
- **Unknown** when the trio genuinely does not have enough evidence to assess. This is an evidence gap to investigate, not a failure.
- **Not applicable** when the criterion does not structurally apply (e.g., competitive landscape for an internal tool)

Every "strong", "medium", or "weak" assessment should trace back to at least one specific opportunity quote from the interviews. If the rationale cannot point to a concrete quote, the trio probably does not have enough evidence and "unknown" is the honest call.

## Keep effort out of the comparison

Per Torres's principle: "Don't assess effort during opportunity selection." Score every criterion as if implementation were free.

Build complexity, scalability, integration risk, and similar feasibility dimensions do not belong here. If the conversation drifts toward "but it would be hard to build", pull it back to the criterion being discussed. Effort is assessed later during assumption testing, not here.

## What the comparison produces

The comparison gives the trio three things:

1. **A shared mental model** of how the opportunities differ on each criterion. Not a ranking, but a picture of trade-offs.
2. **An evidence-gap list** of the "unknown" cells: where does the trio need more customer evidence before it can decide?
3. **A basis for selection.** The trio picks an opportunity to pursue based on the full picture, not a formula.

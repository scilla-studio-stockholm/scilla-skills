# Research questions

A research question is what the team wants to learn from customers. An interview question is what the interviewer says out loud to get it.[^1] This is a practical guide to writing the research questions before an interview round, with a worked example.

The interview itself, the opening question and the follow-ups, is in the story-based interviews reference.

## The step before the interview

A research question helps the trio plan a productive interview. It says what the team needs to learn to move its product outcome or explore its opportunity, and the interview guide is built to get those answers inside one customer story.

The chain runs: business outcome, product outcome, research questions, opening question, follow-ups. The outcome sets the scope of the research questions.[^2] A team with a directional outcome and a wide opportunity space asks wider questions than a team with a specific outcome and a chosen opportunity. The same interview opener can serve both, with the follow-ups going deeper in different places.

## A worked example

A team working on a video streaming product wants to understand viewing habits and writes down what it needs to learn:[^9]

> What do you like to watch?
> Where do you watch?
> Who do you watch with?
> What device do you watch on?

These are the research questions. One viewing session contains answers to all four, so the opener asks for one:

> Tell me about the last time you watched Netflix.

The follow-ups walk that session in time order: "What happened first?", "What happened next?", "What happened before that?". "Is that typical?" tells the team whether the story is representative.[^10] The interviewer listens for the four research questions inside the story.

The outcome sets the scope. A team whose outcome concerns existing viewers opens with the last time they watched. A team whose outcome is winning people who do not watch yet opens with the last time they did something fun.[^11] Once the team has chosen a target opportunity it asks the same opener and goes deeper on the part of the story that concerns it.

The opener usually takes this shape, in whatever language the interview runs: "Berätta om när du letade efter en barnvakt första gången".

## What makes a research question usable

- It is answerable by listening to what people did. "How do families with school-age children decide how to spend money on vacations?" can be answered from one vacation story.[^3] "Will people buy or use our product in the future?" cannot be answered by any interview.[^4]
- It names a behaviour or a moment, so the interviewer knows what to listen for. "Where do you watch?" names a place the story passes through. A question about how people experience a whole process names nothing to listen for until it is refined into what they do in it.
- It is tied to a decision the team has to make. "If you get an unexpected answer to a question and it doesn't affect what you're doing, it wasn't a terribly important question."[^5] The check runs backwards from the outcome: which answer would change what the team builds next?
- It is not written to confirm a solution the team already has.[^6]
- It is short enough to hold. One source puts the count at three per type of person, chosen as "the 3 questions which seem murkiest or most important right now".[^7] The list above has four.

## From the list to the guide

Take the list and ask which single story would contain the answers to all of it.[^8] That story is the opening question: the last time, the first time, or the time something went wrong. The follow-ups then walk the story in time order and stop at the places the research questions point to.

Two things to check before the team goes and interviews:

- The opening question asks for one specific past event, with a time anchor, and contains neither the team's solution nor a research question as written.
- An inexperienced interviewer has a cheat sheet of follow-ups to rely on: "what happened next", "what did you do then", "who else was involved", "is that typical", and the temporal prompts that take the story backwards and forwards. The sheet keeps the interview on the story.

The interview planner produces the guide itself: the intro script, the opening question, the conditional follow-ups, and a coverage checklist that maps each research question to where in the story it gets answered.

Aligning each interview question to a research question, and the questions interviews cannot answer. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interview-guide/ and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-user-interviews-fail/

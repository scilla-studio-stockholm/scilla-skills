# Definition of "Product" in a Product Operating Model

"Product" is one of those words that is used often but means different things depending on the context. Here we aim to understand Product in the context of the Product Operating Model.

That gap of understanding is a risk in the sense of being able to move to the Product Operating Model. It might limit the possibilities to execute within the Product Operating Model for those involved in a product team, or for those enabling the product organisation. This article aims to explain what Product is, within the Product Operating Model.

## A note on scope

This article describes product in the context of tech-enabled products: software, platforms, digital services, and increasingly, physical products with a digital layer. It does not cover purely physical goods. But the line is moving: as physical products become more tech-enabled, the thinking here applies to more of them.

## What Product is not

None of these things are, on their own, *the product*, in the sense of Product Operating Model and therefore not the limitation of what a product team is responsible for:

- A feature
- A piece of software
- An API
- A dataset

They can all be parts of a product. But *the product* is something larger.

## This is what Product is, in the Product Operating Model

An interconnected system of all the following:[^1]

- the functionality / features
- the technology that enables them
- the digital user experience that helps users interact with the functionality
- how to monetize them
- how to enable distribution to attract and acquire users
- and other potential touchpoints the company engineers to deliver its value proposition.

## The counterintuitive examples

Two of Cagan's examples are worth holding onto, because sometimes they are counterintuitive:

> For an e-commerce business, product includes everything except the actual merchandise being sold. For a media company, product is everything except the content.

This is how Cagan distinguishes between Product (tech-enabled software products) and their underlying Assets.

## The nature of a product

### 1. Product delivers value repeatedly without being rebuilt each time

A product "delivers value repeatedly to customers and users, without requiring the company to build something new every time."[^4] The same built thing serves the next customer at close to no extra build cost, and the whole product case rests on that. Rebuild it for each customer and you have a services business, which can be a good business and is a different one.

### 2. Product is a value exchange between two parts

Product is not a one-way delivery. Customers have problems. Businesses create products that solve them. The customer realizes value only when their problem is solved, and only then do they provide sustained value back to the business. Both sides must work. Customer value: does it solve the problem? Business value: does it work for the business?

### 3. Changes in one area, creates ripples in all the other areas of product

Since product is not a collection of independent features, every change in one end creates second and third-order effects. Market, product, channels, and business model form a loop, each constraining and shaping the others.[^2]

### 4. Product is never finished

A product is not a project. Projects have endpoints. Products do not. A product is continuously discovered through close contact with customers. The question is never "did we build it?", it's "did the behavior change? did the value materialize?"

## What also counts as part of the product

Functionality, technology and the user experience are usually assumed to be the product team's responsibility but we need to be clear on what also needs to be included and thought of as "the product" since decisions in both areas have an effect on what and how the product is built and experienced by the users.

### 5. Product molds around and can also shape the business model

You cannot separate the product from how it makes money. Monetization is not something that happens after the product is built. It is part of how the product is built. The pricing model, how its sold and how its purchased and whether customer acquisition is economically viable. All of the above impacts product and tech decisions.

### 6. Product molds and should also shape how it's distributed (the growth model)

How the product reaches customers is a product problem, as much as a marketing problem. Products must be designed and technically fit for their distribution channels. A product with aligned distribution and average quality will outperform a beautiful product with broken distribution. Product growth is about distribution, and support for the distribution channels needs to be built into the product to work. "Build it and they will come" does not apply anymore.

## How we increase our odds of success

### 7. Product starts from the customer, not from the solution

The market comes first. The problem comes first. Product works outside-in, understanding the customer's problem space before starting product design or building things. Then continuously validate towards customers as we improve and build more.

### 8. Product is measured by outcomes, not outputs

An output is what gets shipped: a feature, a release, a line of code. An outcome is what changes in the world because of it: a behavior, a decision, a business result. A product does not exist because features were delivered on time. It exists because something changed for the customer and for the business. If nothing changed, there is no product, only activity.

## Internal products and platform products

This definition holds also for internal products and platform products. Read "customer" as whoever receives the value: internal users or a team consuming a platform. Two points that can be overlooked for internal and platform products are usually the business model and how it's distributed.

## What it means to be "a product person"

When someone says they are a product person, they mean they think about all of this at once. Not just the features or the backlog, but the whole system: who the customer is, what problem is worth solving, how the solution reaches people, how it creates value for them, and how that value sustains the business. A product person holds four risks in mind at once: whether customers will find the product valuable, whether users can actually use it, whether engineering can build it, and whether it works for the business (is viable). They hold the tension between customer needs and business constraints, between what is desirable and what is viable, between shipping now and learning more. It is not a role. It is a way of thinking about the work. A product person can be an analyst, a product manager, a researcher, an engineer, a designer. It's not a role, it's a mindset.

## What it means to be "a product team" in the Product Operating Model

A product team is a team that owns the product in this full sense. They do not receive a list of features to build or sit at their desks and come up with solutions in isolation. In the full sense of POM, they receive a problem or an outcome, and they figure out how to solve it. That means they do discovery: they talk to customers, they identify opportunities, they test assumptions. And they do delivery: they build, ship, and measure whether value was actually created. Both, continuously. A product team is accountable for outcomes, not output. If they shipped everything on time but nothing changed for the customer, they did not succeed.

A product team wants to build successful products. POM's conviction is that a team gets there by learning how to approach the four risks. The methods come from people who lived the alternative, where teams are handed roadmaps and judged on delivery dates. That model manages one risk, the schedule. Hitting the date counts as proof that value was created. An organisation moves into the product model only once its leadership accepts that the other four risks are real and that they cost money.

## Further reading

- Article: Brian Balfour - ["Four Fits for $100M+ Growth"](https://brianbalfour.com/four-fits-growth-framework). Can't recommend this article series enough, it's so well written and though through. Defines product as a system: market/product, product/channel, channel/model, model/market fits.
- Article: John Cutler - ["12 Signs You're Working in a Feature Factory"](https://medium.com/@johnpcutler/12-signs-youre-working-in-a-feature-factory-44a5b938d6a2). Defines product by contrasting it. It is easier to explain what product is, by describing what it is not.
- Podcast: *Lenny's Podcast* - ["The nature of product" with Marty Cagan](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-nature-of-product-marty-cagan). A long conversation on what product actually is and how it differs from a feature set.
- Book: Marty Cagan - *[INSPIRED: How to Create Tech Products Customers Love](https://www.svpg.com/books/inspired-how-to-create-tech-products-customers-love-2nd-edition/)*. The foundational articulation of product work and the four product risks: value, usability, feasibility, viability.
- Book: Melissa Perri - *[Escaping the Build Trap](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/escaping-the-build/9781491973783/)*. Defines product by contrast with the project/feature-delivery mindset. Particularly strong on outcomes vs. outputs, and on the sales-led vs product-led contrast.
- Article: Marty Cagan - ["Discovery vs. Delivery"](https://www.svpg.com/discovery-vs-delivery/). Short piece where the requirement is stated plainly: discovery has to produce one solution that works for many customers.
- Article: Marty Cagan - ["Charter Customer Programs"](https://www.svpg.com/charter-customer-programs/). How he operationalises it: six reference customers from a single target market, one solution that works for all six.

[^1]: The list follows Marty Cagan's in *INSPIRED*, chapter 8. He calls it the holistic product. We drop the adjective, because "product" already means this.
[^2]: Brian Balfour's four fits.
[^4]: Melissa Perri, *Escaping the Build Trap*, on what a product is. The second clause is the one that usually gets dropped when the definition is repeated.
